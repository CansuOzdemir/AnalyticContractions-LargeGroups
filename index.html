Analytic Contractions and Their Large Groups

Cansu Özdemir

Supervisor: Prof. Ilia Binder · University of Toronto

Welcome. Today: what a contraction of a Lie group is, how analytic contractions let us pick the contracted group, and İnönü’s large group. We start with the physics that motivated all of this.

Motivation

Two friends meet at a café

You

Slow compared to light: classical mechanics

Your friend

Moves close to the speed of light c

As c → ∞ the two equations become the same.

Can we take the same limit of their symmetries?

Unless you have special powers, you live in classical mechanics. A friend who can move close to the speed of light lives in relativistic mechanics. The equations of motion are related by a limit: c → ∞ turns the relativistic one into Newton’s law. The question of the talk: does the same work for symmetries?

Two symmetry groups

Galilei and Poincaré: same shape, different boosts

Classical: GalileiRelativistic: Poincaré
Time translations (1)t′ = t + st′ = t + s
Space translations (3)x′ = x + ax′ = x + a
Rotations (3)x′ = Rx, R ∈ SO(3)x′ = Rx, R ∈ SO(3)
Boosts (3)t′ = t, x′ = x − vtt′ = γ(t − vx/c²), x′ = γ(x − vt)
Dimension1010

Boosts along the x-axis, with γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²).

The symmetries of both worlds: time and space translations, rotations and boosts. Everything agrees except the boosts. Both groups are 10-dimensional.

Their Lie algebras

Only two brackets differ

H, P, J and K generate time translations, space translations, rotations and boosts.

Only the boost–boost and boost–translation brackets change.

At the level of Lie algebras the difference is even smaller: only two brackets differ, the boost–boost and the boost–translation brackets. In Galilei they are 0.

The problem

Same dimension. Not subgroups of each other. No c inside the groups.

So how do we take the limit c → ∞ of a group? İnönü and Wigner, 1953: group contraction.

Both groups have dimension 10, neither is a subgroup of the other, and the groups themselves do not contain c. So it is not clear what a limit of groups should be. This is the question group contractions answer.

Applications in physics

Spacetime and gravity

Kinematics

All kinematical groups are contractions of the de Sitter groups; c → 0 gives the Carroll group.

Bacry & Lévy-Leblond 1968 · Lévy-Leblond 1965

Newtonian gravity

Keeping one order beyond the contraction, a Lie algebra expansion, gives Newtonian gravity an action.

Hansen, Hartong & Obers 2019 · Bergshoeff et al. 2019

Flat space holography

The symmetry at null infinity is a contracted conformal symmetry; the proposed dual is a Carrollian CFT.

Bagchi 2010 · Donnay et al. 2022 · Bagchi et al. 2026

Black holes

The symmetries near a black hole horizon are c → 0 limits of relativistic ones.

Donnay & Marteau 2019

İnönü and Wigner’s c → ∞ was only the beginning. Today contractions appear wherever a theory has a limit. The opposite limit c → 0 gives the Carroll group, which is now central in flat space holography and near black hole horizons. And keeping one more order than the contraction, a Lie algebra expansion, is what gives Newtonian gravity an action principle.

Applications in physics

Strings, matter and lattices

Strings

Non-relativistic and tensionless strings are obtained from string theory by contractions.

Gomis & Ooguri 2001 · Bagchi et al. 2026

Condensed matter

Fractons and flat-band systems, such as magic-angle graphene, have Carroll symmetry.

Figueroa-O’Farrill, Pérez & Prohazka 2023 · Bagchi et al. 2023

Lattice models

Contracting su(2) of the Heisenberg XXZ model gives iso(2) for the XY model and the Heisenberg algebra h₃ for the Ising model.

Ongoing work

Contractions also give non-relativistic and tensionless strings, Carroll symmetry in fractons and flat bands, and in ongoing work they connect three lattice models. They also have a long history in harmonic analysis: the title of İnönü and Wigner’s 1953 paper is “On the contraction of groups and their representations”. We come back to this at the end of the talk.

Seeing a contraction

SO(3) → E(2): the sphere becomes its tangent plane

Sphere of radius 1/c(ε). Orange: a point at distance 2 on the sphere. Blue ring: the same point on the tangent plane.

Contract SO(3) with respect to the rotations X_1 around the north pole: φ_ε(X_1) = X_1 and φ_ε(X_2,3) = c(ε)X_2,3, so only [X_2, X_3]_ε = c(ε)² X_1 changes, and in the limit X_2, X_3 commute and become the translations of E(2). Geometrically, SO(3) acts on a sphere of radius 1/c(ε); the generators X_2, X_3 scaled by c(ε) move the north pole a fixed distance, so contracting is making the sphere bigger and looking only near the north pole. Left: c(ε) = ε, an analytic contraction. Right: c(ε) = exp(1 − 1/ε²), a non-analytic one with the same limit. Same clock ε on both sides, starting from the same unit sphere at ε = 1. The analytic sphere flattens gradually; the non-analytic one is already flat by ε ≈ 0.5, and its curvature c² has Taylor series 0 at ε = 0. So the Lie algebra expansions g_n see ε² on the left but only zeros on the right: they cannot tell that the right family came from a sphere.

Khasanov and Kuperstein

Contractions through bundles

Back at the café. Khasanov and Kuperstein put all the brackets together into one object, the İnönü–Wigner bundle: over every ε in [0, 1] the same vector space, here so(3) with φ_ε the identity on h = span X_1 and ε on X_2, X_3, so only [X_2, X_3]_ε = ε² X_1 changes. A section picks one element in every fibre, and sections are bracketed fibre by fibre: for b(ε) = X_2 + εX_1 and c(ε) = X_3 we get [b, c](ε) = ε² X_1 − ε X_2. Evaluating at 0 is a surjective homomorphism onto g_0 with kernel εg̃. Here [b, c] vanishes at 0, so it lies in εg̃, and in g_0 ≅ g̃/εg̃ this is [X_2, X_3]_0 = 0. Also b and the constant section X_2 differ by εX_1, so they are the same element of g_0.

Khasanov and Kuperstein

The quotient g̃ / εg̃

g̃ is the Lie algebra of all analytic sections of the İnönü–Wigner bundle, with the fibrewise bracket. It is infinite-dimensional, and so is εg̃, the sections that vanish at 0. Two sections have the same value at 0 exactly when their difference lies in εg̃. Dividing out εg̃ is sliding every section to its value at 0: sections with the same value merge, but no point of the fibre over 0 is lost. What is left is one constant section for every point of the fibre over 0, so g̃/εg̃ ≅ g_0 is 3-dimensional, a full copy of g: a contraction does not lose dimension, it only changes the bracket. Evaluation at 0 is a Lie algebra homomorphism, so the bracket comes along: [b, c] = ε²X_1 − εX_2 ∈ εg̃ gives [X_2, X_3]_0 = 0.

Khasanov and Kuperstein

Lie algebra expansions: g2

The same picture one order higher. g_2 = g̃/ε³g̃ keeps the value at 0 and the first two derivatives of a section. Dividing out ε³g̃ slides every section to its Taylor polynomial of degree 2, a_0 + εa_1 + ε²a_2: sections that agree to second order merge, by Hadamard's lemma applied three times. So as a vector space g_2 ≅ g ⊕ εg ⊕ ε²g, of dimension 9 for so(3). It is strictly more than g_0: the blue and purple classes have the same value at 0, so they are the same element of g_0, but different elements of g_2. And g_2 remembers the sphere: for the constant sections X_2, X_3 the bracket is [X_2, X_3]_ε = ε²X_1. In g_0 this is 0, since ε²X_1 ∈ εg̃; in g_2 it is not, since ε²X_1 ∉ ε³g̃. For the non-analytic family c(ε) = exp(1 − 1/ε²) the bracket c(ε)²X_1 has all derivatives 0 at 0, so it lies in ε³g̃ (in fact in every ε^n g̃) and every g_n sees only e(2).

Khasanov and Kuperstein

Lie algebra expansions: gn for n = 0, …, 5

For curiosity: the classes in g_n = g̃/ε^(n+1)g̃ are the Taylor polynomials of degree n of the sections. The animation steps through n = 0, …, 5: constants, lines, parabolas, cubics and so on, and the coloured curves get closer and closer to the dashed sections themselves. The dimension is 3(n+1). The green curve is the bracket of the constant sections X_2 and X_3, [X_2, X_3]_ε = ε²X_1: it is 0 in g_0 and g_1 and appears from n = 2 on. For the İnönü-Wigner family c(ε) = ε the brackets are exactly μ_0 + ε²μ_2, so after n = 2 the g_n only grow in dimension and carry no new information about the family; for the non-analytic family every g_n sees only e(2).

Prinz, Schmeding and Schwartz

Contractions through germs

Two bundles over [1, 0], drawn schematically: every rod is a fibre, and we only draw the X_2, X_3-direction, because φ_ε does not change the h-part. Top: the İnönü–Wigner bundle, with sections c̃. Bottom: the trivial bundle g × [1, 0], with g and its original bracket in every fibre; this is where the germs of Prinz, Schmeding and Schwartz live. φ_ε maps the top fibre onto g and squeezes the X_2, X_3-direction by ε, so the ticks on the bottom rods move together, and at ε = 0 everything lands in h. A germ in Γ_φ(g) is exactly a section pushed down fibre by fibre: c(ε) = φ_ε(c̃(ε)). All germs end in h, but their sections end all over the top fibre over 0, so c̃(0) remembers what c(0) forgets. Dividing out εΓ_φ(g) identifies germs whose sections agree at 0, exactly as on the previous slide, and the classes fill the whole fibre over 0: Γ_φ(g)/εΓ_φ(g) ≅ g_0, again a full copy of g.

Prinz, Schmeding and Schwartz

The quotient Γφ(g) / εΓφ(g)

Why the quotient is needed, and why it works. Take the germs c(ε) = φ_ε(c̃(ε)) for the same sections as on the quotient slide for the bundle. In g their values at 0 only fill h, because φ_0(g) = h: if we only looked at c(0), the cube would be squashed to a segment and two dimensions would be lost. Pulling back by φ_ε⁻¹ gives the sections c̃ again, and the cube opens up. Dividing out εΓ_φ(g), the germs whose c̃ vanishes at 0, identifies germs whose c̃ agree at 0, and what is left is again the whole fibre over 0: Γ_φ(g)/εΓ_φ(g) ≅ g_0, three-dimensional, a full copy of g. The bracket works too: computed in g with its own bracket, [φ_ε(X_2 + εX_1), φ_ε(X_3)] = ε²X_1 − ε²X_2 = φ_ε(ε²X_1 − εX_2), which lies in εΓ_φ(g), so [X_2, X_3]_0 = 0, as for the bundle.

Prinz, Schmeding and Schwartz

Germs in the group: SO(3) → E(2)

The same idea one level up. A germ is a curve f(ε) of rotations of the sphere with f(0) = k in H, a rotation about the north pole N. Left: the sphere itself, at a fixed size. Each germ moves the letter F, and as ε → 0 every germ shrinks back to N: at scale 1 only the rotation k is left. Right: the small dashed window around N, magnified by 1/ε. There each germ still goes somewhere: the letter converges to a rigid motion of the plane, turn by k and move by a, where f(ε) = exp(εa + O(ε²))k. The dashed orange germ differs from the solid one only at order ε², so it is in the same class and has the same limit; the green one is in another class. So the classes are the elements (k, a) of E(2), and products of germs become (k_1 k_2, a_1 + k_1 a_2), the multiplication of SO(2) ⋉ ℝ²: Γ(G)_H / Γ(G)_H^(1) ≅ E(2).

Harmonic analysis · 1 of 4

Legendre polynomials are matrix entries of SO(3)

The spin ℓ representation π_ℓ is the space of spherical harmonics of degree ℓ: the eigenfunctions of the Laplacian of the unit sphere with eigenvalue −ℓ(ℓ+1). It has dimension 2ℓ + 1, with the basis Y_ℓ^m, m = −ℓ, …, ℓ, where m says how Y_ℓ^m turns under rotations about the north pole N. A rotation g acts by (π_ℓ(g)Y)(y) = Y(g⁻¹y), so it has a (2ℓ+1)×(2ℓ+1) matrix in this basis. For R_β, the rotation by β about a horizontal axis, the entries are the Wigner functions d^ℓ_mn(β). The middle entry uses Y_ℓ^0 = Z, the function that only depends on the height: move Z by g and measure how much of it is left; this is P_ℓ(cos θ). The animation shows this middle entry for ℓ = 2. For ℓ = 1: Z(y) = y·N, π(g)Z(y) = y·gN, and the overlap is cos θ.

Harmonic analysis · 2 of 4

Bessel functions are matrix entries of E(2)

ρ: the frequency of the plane waves (Laplacian eigenvalue −ρ²). a ∈ ℝ²: the translation. Pairing with 1 is averaging: ⟨f, 1⟩ = (1/2π)∫ f dφ.

The plane analogue. π_ρ acts on functions on the circle of directions u = (cos φ, sin φ): the translation by a multiplies by the plane wave e^(iρ a·u), the rotation k turns the circle. Plane waves of frequency ρ are eigenfunctions of the Laplacian of the plane with eigenvalue −ρ², the analogue of −ℓ(ℓ+1). The basis e_m = e^(imφ) plays the role of Y_ℓ^m, and m again says how it turns under rotations. The matrix of the translation by a = (0, a) has the entries J_(m−n)(ρa): this is the Jacobi–Anger expansion e^(ix sin φ) = Σ J_k(x) e^(ikφ). The middle entry, e_0 = 1, is the average of all plane waves, J_0(ρa); the animation shows this average being built up.

Talman’s example · 3 of 4

Contracting the generators: the spin has to grow

J± = Jx ± iJy: the ladder operators of X2, X3. Equivalently, the Laplacian of the sphere of radius 1/ε: −ε²ℓ(ℓ+1) → −ρ².

Talman, Section 11-8. Near N, small rotations about the horizontal axes act like translations, so X_2, X_3 are multiplied by ε (Talman’s 1/c) and the brackets become those of e(2). In the basis Y_ℓ^m the ladder operators J_± = J_x ± iJ_y have entries √((ℓ∓m)(ℓ±m+1)). Times ε they go to 0 if ℓ is fixed, and converge exactly when εℓ → ρ; the limit ρ is the entry of the translation generators in π_ρ. Talman takes ℓ the integer closest to ρc. Equivalently −ε²ℓ(ℓ+1) → −ρ².

Talman’s example · 4 of 4

Rotations become translations: the whole matrix converges

Rotation by εa about a horizontal axis ↦ translation by a. Jn−m are the entries of πρ in the basis (−1)mem.

The group level, Talman’s formula in Section 11-8, which he attributes to İnönü and Wigner. The rotation by the angle εa about a horizontal axis moves N by the distance a on the sphere of radius 1/ε, so in the limit it is the translation by a. Its matrix in π_ℓ, the d^ℓ_mn(εa), converges entry by entry to the matrix of the translation in π_ρ. The reason: both are one-parameter groups, and by the previous slide their generators converge. Left: the 5×5 middle block of the rotation matrix for growing ℓ with εℓ = 1 and a = 2. Middle: the translation matrix, J_(n−m)(ρa). Right: the middle row as functions of a. The middle entry is Mehler–Heine: P_ℓ(cos εa) → J_0(ρa). So the Legendre polynomials go to J_0, and the other Wigner functions go to the other Bessel functions.
